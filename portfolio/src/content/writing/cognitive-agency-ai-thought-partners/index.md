## The Problem

Technology has always had a rocky place in education. With each digital addition—calculators, projectors, laptops, and now AI—parents and educators have always had some skepticism about how to incorporate and regulate these capabilities into students' teaching resources. This criticism has become even more striking with the rise of AI: **the inverse relationship between access to information and critical thinking poses an existential threat to the very foundational skills of education**. Over the past few years, unregulated AI use has prompted students to expedite assignments, including problem sets and essays, by leveraging LLMs as shortcuts at every step of the writing process, from early brainstorming to final editing.

And though educators have long been wary of plagiarism, AI poses a more sinister threat, pre-empting a student's ability to think critically. When learning how to think—writing an essay, solving a complex problem, building understanding—people improve not by just getting answers, but by sitting with confusion, hitting dead ends, reorganizing their understanding of the problem, and suddenly seeing it differently and with a more robust understanding than before.

> The most salient memory I have of this was from my senior year in high school. Having read Billy Collins's "Introduction to Poetry," I was tasked with writing a thesis arguing the author's meaning with three supporting arguments. There wasn't a dearth of possibilities - rather the opposite; every few minutes, I would walk over to the teacher's desk with a new draft. Four hours later, we'd gone through dozens of drafts, identifying and replacing unfounded assumptions. She would read it and say, "This doesn't quite make sense here. What are you actually trying to say?" She would intentionally withhold the correct answer, forcing me to reconcile my own arguments and clarify my own thinking. We continued this feedback cycle for several hours until the writing was finally refined - though inefficient, the struggle was where the learning happened.

This isn't a rare moment of productive friction; there's real cognitive science evidence to back it up. A 2025 paper by Dr. Kosmyna at the MIT Media Lab studied the immediate impacts of technology-assisted writing across nine SAT Writing prompts and three groups: one with no assistance ("brain only"), one with access to the web ("search engine"), and one with access to large language models (LLMs). They concluded that "brain connectivity systematically scaled down with the amount of external support: the Brain‑only group exhibited the strongest, widest‑ranging networks, Search Engine group showed intermediate engagement, and LLM assistance elicited the weakest overall coupling." We are already seeing evidence that regular use of LLMs significantly reduces neural activity, just a few years after the advent of commercial AI. While the long-term effects have yet to be fully documented, the consequences could be far more severe if we fail to design effective guardrails against overreliance.

This shortcut threatens the core mechanics of insight. In their 2006 study, Doctors Wiley and Ash established that breakthroughs arise from a specific, non-linear sequence: we misrepresent a problem, struggle in that flawed mental space, and are forced to restructure our thinking. AI eliminates the first half, and **without the struggle of the search, the cognitive restructuring never happens**.

The overarching risk is that if AI thought partners are designed to be helpful by providing answers, frameworks, and quick resolutions, they allow users to bypass the real struggle in the faulty-representation and solution-search phase that builds deep thinking in favor of short-term productivity gains. In the context of education, a student uses AI to draft an essay and completes it faster—but never reaches the moment when their assumptions about the topic are repeatedly questioned and revised, building depth not only in what they are thinking but also in how they're coming to these conclusions. They've outsourced the underlying mental work that would have built their capacity to think.

This is cognitive offloading at a critical developmental stage - we're enabling aspiring young thinkers to outsource the struggle that builds the ability to learn.

## Why It Matters Now

We are currently in the early stages of an intellectual revolution: technology corporations are deploying AI at scale, and students are using it—with or without permission. The question educators and parents face isn't "should students use AI?" It's "how do we help students use AI in ways that preserve their cognitive agency?"

Recent work in cognitive science has begun to explore what a thoughtful human-AI partnership actually requires. Collins et al. (2024) in Nature Human Behavior lay out what genuine thought partners should:

1. Understand their partner: the machine should adapt itself to the partner's goals, plans, (possibly false) beliefs, and limitations (attention, level of expertise)
2. Is legible to their partner: the machine's responses should neatly fit in their partner's understanding of the world, using an intuitive shared language or symbols
3. Share an understanding of the world: the machine should share accurate and relevant information

The risk we're identifying here is that current thought partners optimize for helpfulness in ways that may undermine the very cognitive development they're meant to support. In the pursuit of sharing relevant and accurate information the first time, chatbots are effectively disabling a student's ability to scrutinize their own understanding of a problem, and, in doing so, any knowledge they acquire is encoded only superficially.

Such a widely accessible innovation cannot be easily contained, but we can help educators and parents understand what's at stake: not whether students finish assignments, but whether they're building the capacity to think independently and sit with hard problems. Most importantly, they are unknowingly risking their ability to recognize when and how to recognize when their assumptions break down.
